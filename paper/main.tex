  \textbf{Attributing Rotary Position Embeddings: Exact Score Attribution,
  and What Actually Limits It}%
  \vspace{-1.5ex}}

\author{Ilya Slyatski}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Rotary position embeddings (RoPE) \citep{su2021ropeformer} are the default
positional encoding in most modern transformers, and interpretability work now
routinely attributes attention scores to learned features using an additive
decomposition --- the task now called QK attribution
\citep{kamath2025tracing}. We take the structure of that decomposition
seriously and ask a
narrow question: \emph{exactly} what does RoPE do to it? Three things. First,
for a fixed pair of positions, the RoPE attention score is exactly bilinear in
the content vectors, so the additive feature decomposition
$\mathrm{score} = \sum_{ij} f_i g_j A_{ij}(\delta)$ is exact, not approximate;
we measure the residual at $3.9\times10^{-14}$. Because the rotary transform is
orthogonal, query and key magnitudes are exactly position-independent; the
per-pair closed form then separates a content-only amplitude from a
distance-only angle. Second, the exactness
does not extend to position-\emph{free} coefficients: $A_{ij}$ is a function of
the relative distance $\delta$, and we measure one feature's contribution
reversing sign across the distance range while
the total remains additive to $5.0\times10^{-14}$. A position-free per-feature
attribution number is therefore not well-posed under RoPE; the per-feature,
per-distance one is. Third, we measure what context extension actually buys.
YaRN \citep{peng2023yarn} keeps $\mathrm{base} = 10000$ and ramps per frequency;
consequently $\max_k|\D|$ is bit-identical to plain RoPE at every one of $54$
tested distances, while the median angle falls by a constant factor of $0.4464$
and the linearizable fraction rises. Position interpolation
\citep{chen2023positionalinterp} and the common ``raise the base'' heuristic are
measured as controls. The honest conclusion is that context extension buys a
larger linearizable \emph{majority}, not a safe global linearization of the
head, because a few fast channels dominate the error. Partial RoPE is the clean
exception: at $p = 0.25$ the unrotated sub-score is position-free to exactly
zero spread. We then carry the attribution from synthetic vectors to a public
$32$k dictionary on \texttt{pythia-70m}, where the decomposition stays exact to
$3.1\times10^{-14}$: in the rotary subspace the best position-free scalar
mis-signs $22.4\%$ of material cells, while the total profile --- dominated by
the $48$ of $64$ channels RoPE never rotates --- mis-signs $0.03\%$. Finally we
price the same substitution at the attention level: even the
least-squares-optimal constant, fitted per query with knowledge of the exact
profile, changes the argmax key for $27\%$--$40\%$ of queries and leaves
$57\%$--$68\%$ top-5 overlap on three $135$--$160$M checkpoints. All numbers are
computed by the implementation in this repository against committed artifacts;
this is a structural characterization, and we state explicitly what it does not
include.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Feature-level attribution has become a working tool in mechanistic
interpretability. Given a decomposition of an activation into a sum of learned
features, one asks how much each feature contributed to a circuit's output.
Applied to attention, this is QK attribution, and it runs into a structural
question that is usually glossed over: attention scores are computed between a
query at one position and a key at another, and under a positional encoding,
``how much did feature $i$ contribute'' is not obviously a scalar.

Under RoPE it is not a scalar, and the reason is specific. In this paper we
derive the score in the relative frame, where RoPE's orthogonality makes the
query unrotated and the key rotated by $\R_{\delta}$, $\delta = n - m$.
Expanding that inner product channel by channel yields an exact closed form: the
head decomposes into $\half$ rotary pairs, each contributing a single sinusoid
in $\delta$ whose amplitude is fixed by the content vectors and whose phase
moves linearly with distance. The amplitude is a \emph{gate}; the phase is the
whole of the position dependence. This is a cleaner object than the score
itself, and it is exactly computable.

Three results follow, and we state them as precisely as we can.

\paragraph{R1: attribution is exact, and the magnitudes are position-free.}
For a fixed position pair the score is exactly bilinear in $(q, k)$, so the
additive decomposition holds to $3.9\times10^{-14}$. Moreover, because
$\R_{\delta}$ is orthogonal, $|q|$ and $|k|$ are exactly
position-independent. Any claim that RoPE corrupts bilinearity, or smears the
magnitude with position, is false at the score level, and we measure it to be
false rather than asserting it.

\paragraph{R2: the real obstacle is position-conditionality, not bilinearity.}
$A_{ij}$ depends on $\delta$. A single feature's contribution is a function of
distance, not a number. We measure that every feature's contribution reverses sign across a
$54$-point distance grid, against an additivity residual of
$5.0\times10^{-14}$. The well-posed object under RoPE is a per-feature,
per-distance attribution. A position-free scalar per feature is not. On real
dictionary features the reversal reappears at $22.4\%$ of material cells --- but
only in the rotary channels: the total profile reverses on $0.03\%$ of cells,
because the unrotated channels dominate it. Both numbers are needed, and the
second is what conceals the first from a reader of a total-attribution table.

\paragraph{R3: context extension buys a majority, not a global linearization.}
The common story is that RoPE's angles grow without bound and methods fix it by
damping them. Measuring $\max_k |\D|$, $\operatorname{median}_k |\D|$ and the
fraction of channels admitting $\cos \D \mapsto 1 - \D^2/2$,
$\sin \D \mapsto \D$ across RoPE, \yarn, position interpolation and the
``$\mathrm{base} = 500000$'' heuristic, we find $\max_k |\D|$ is
\emph{identical} for RoPE and \yarn\ at every distance tested. \yarn\
deliberately leaves the fastest channel alone. It lowers the median and raises
the linearizable fraction, but the amplitude-weighted error ratio
\yarn/RoPE is $0.99684$ at $\delta = 8192$. The fast channels dominate the
error and \yarn\ does not touch them.

\subsection{What we do not claim}

This matters enough to state before the results. Most of the algebra is measured
on synthetic query and key vectors from a fixed
seed, with $8$ random directions and non-negative coefficients: a
test fixture for the algebra, not a model of real feature geometry.
Section~\ref{sec:trained} repeats the central measurements on activations from
three trained checkpoints, and Section~\ref{sec:features} adds one public $32$k
dictionary on a fourth --- an existence proof that the structure survives real
features, not a population estimate, and not evidence that its units are the ones
a circuit uses. There is no retrieval benchmark and no accuracy measurement: no
perplexity, no needle-in-a-haystack score, no long-context result. Where we
compare RoPE and \yarn\ we compare \emph{quantities of the position encoding} ---
angles, linearizable fractions, error ratios. We do not claim any scheme is
better in practice: all are in real use, our table ranks them on angle geometry
only, and Section~\ref{sec:context} shows that a favourable ranking on one axis
does not transfer to the amplitude-weighted one. The algebraic conclusions are
model-independent by construction, which is deliberate: they are claims about
the structure of the score under a positional encoding, and they hold for any
head using it.

The contribution, stated exactly: a correct, carefully measured characterization
of what RoPE does to feature-level attention attribution, an identification of
position-conditionality as the operative obstruction, and a measurement of the
actual effect of context extension on the quantities that determine whether a
head can be linearized.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Setup}

Let $d$ be an even head dimension and $x_m$ the pre-projection content at
absolute position $m$. RoPE assigns channel $k < \half$ the frequency
$\invf_k = \mathrm{base}^{-2k/d}$ ($\theta_0 = \mathrm{base}^{0} = 1$ at the
fastest channel), and rotates the pair $(k, k + \half)$ by $m\cdot\invf_k$; the
base is a design choice with a spectral reading \citep{liu2026oscillator}. With
$q = W_Q x_m$ and $k = W_K x_n$ taken before rotation, the score is

\begin{equation}
  s(m,n) = q^{\!\top}\R_{\delta} k, \qquad \delta = n - m,
  \label{eq:relative}
\end{equation}

which depends on the two absolute positions only through their difference.
Three exact facts follow, and they are what the rest of the paper uses: the
norms $|\R_\delta k| = |k|$ carry no position information
(Proposition~\ref{prop:gate}); $A_k$, $B_k$, $R_k$ and $\psik$ are functions of
the content vectors alone and $c_k$ is a non-constant sinusoid in $\delta$
whenever $R_k > 0$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}); and a feature's contribution
is $\delta$-independent only if every rotary pair it touches vanishes
(Proposition~\ref{prop:cond}). The frequency ladder, the relative-frame
factorisation, the per-pair closed form and the three proofs are in
Appendix~\ref{app:setup}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Where the non-additivity actually is}
\label{sec:softmax}

It is worth being precise about what the softmax destroys. Write the attention
weight for key $n$ as
\begin{equation}
  a_n \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\bigl(s(\delta_n)/\sqrt{d}\bigr)}
  {\sum_{n'} \exp\!\bigl(s(\delta_{n'})/\sqrt{d}\bigr)} .
\end{equation}
Two separate operations happen here. The first is the map $n \mapsto
s(\delta_n)$, which by Equation~\ref{eq:additive} is exactly additive in the
features: $s(\delta_n) = \sum_{ij} f_i g_j A_{ij}(\delta_n)$, with the
$\delta_n$ fixed per key. The second is the normalization across $n$, and
\emph{that} is what couples the keys: two keys whose contributions are
individually additive are not jointly additive after division, because the
denominator sums over all of them.

The consequence for attribution: a feature's contribution to the \emph{logits}
is exactly additive and exactly position-conditional, and that is what this
paper measures; its contribution to the attention \emph{weights} is neither, and
the error can grow with the number of keys and the logit spread. An additive
method can be exactly right about the score and still wrong about the weights;
when the weights are the object, the obstruction is the softmax, not the
position encoding.

We can price that second error. For every query of a real passage we compute the
exact score against all causal
keys, and the best position-free surrogate of it: per rotary pair, the
least-squares constant over exactly the distances that query sees, fitted with
knowledge of the exact profile and with the unrotated channels left alone. The
surrogate is optimal by construction, so the damage it does is a lower bound on
the damage of any content-only score. Across every head and prompt of three
$135$--$160$M checkpoints and three original passages of $88$--$122$ tokens,
substituting it changes the argmax key for $27.2\%$--$40.2\%$ of queries, moves
$25.0\%$--$30.9\%$ of the attention mass in total variation, holds top-5 overlap
at $0.57$--$0.68$, and raises the mean forward KL to $0.67$--$2.44$ nats. The
damage concentrates in the early layers --- $0.63$--$0.81$ argmax flips in the
first two layers, against a mean of $0.24$--$0.33$ over the rest --- where logits
are least saturated and a small change moves the maximum. Appendix~\ref{app:attention} gives the
per-layer table, the reconstruction residuals and the provenance.

Table~\ref{tab:exact} establishes the first half of R1: every residual is below
the float64 noise floor, the relative-position property over $42$ pairs, the
orthogonality behind Proposition~\ref{prop:gate} on both query and key, and
bilinearity across $35$ score checks. The per-pair closed form of
Section~\ref{sec:pair} reproduces the brute-force score to the same floor, so the
algebra above is the actual computation, not a post-hoc description of it.

We report these as ceilings rather than digits. Each is the residual of
subtracting two analytically equal expressions, so its value is whatever the
host's summation order produces: the same computation yields $3.6\times10^{-14}$
on one platform and $3.9\times10^{-14}$ on another. The claim that carries
meaning is the bound, and it is checked at test time rather than asserted here.

Two of these deserve a note on how they are tested, because the obvious test is
the wrong one. The relative-position property is \emph{not} tested by comparing
$s(i,j)$ against $s(i + c, j + c)$ with independent content vectors at each
position; that asks whether the score depends on absolute position at all, which
is stronger and false. We test that shifting a \emph{fixed} pair of content vectors together by $7$
positions leaves its score unchanged, over $42$ pairs spanning $7$ distances and
$6$ base positions. Bilinearity is tested by rescaling one side by
$\{0, 1, 2, -3, 7.5, 10^{-3}, 10^{3}\}$ at $5$ distances, giving the $35$ score
checks in the table.

A concrete instance makes the structure visible. The amplitude $\Rk$ of the
fastest rotary pair, $k = 0$, takes the single value $0.9526294617807264$ at
every one of the $54$ distances and under both \rope\ and \yarn\ --- one value,
not fifty-four. That is Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} in the data: the amplitude
is a property of the content vectors, and distance moves only the phase. The
mid-frequency pair $k = 12$ has amplitude $2.3046990966686227$ under the same
key and query, so amplitudes are not interchangeable across pairs either.

The additivity check is the one that matters for attribution. Writing
$q = \sum_i f_i d_i^{(q)}$ and $k = \sum_j g_j d_j^{(k)}$ and expanding both
before rotating, the score decomposes additively:
\begin{equation}
  s(\delta) \;=\; \sum_{i,j} f_i g_j
  \underbrace{q_i^{\!\top}\R_{\delta}k_j}_{A_{ij}(\delta)} .
  \label{eq:additive}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:additive} holds to $3.9\times10^{-14}$ on the $5$-point grid
and $5.0\times10^{-14}$ on the $54$-point grid. Additive attribution to a
sparse-feature code is therefore \emph{exact} at the score level under RoPE, and
any failure mode one wishes to attribute to RoPE's position encoding is not
located here.

The consequence for interpretation is worth stating plainly, because it is the
opposite of a common claim. Bilinearity holds in the score and is destroyed by
the softmax normalization across keys (Section~\ref{sec:softmax}). We therefore
do not claim that attention \emph{output} is additively attributable. It is not,
and RoPE is not why.

\subsection{Position-conditionality is the real obstruction}
\label{sec:cond}
Exact additivity does \emph{not} give position-free coefficients. In
Equation~\ref{eq:additive}, $A_{ij}(\delta)$ carries an explicit $\delta$, even
though the score it sums to depends on $\delta$ alone
(Equation~\ref{eq:relative}). By
Proposition~\ref{prop:cond}, a single feature's contribution
$c_i(\delta) = \sum_j A_{ij}(\delta)$ is a function of distance, and it is
position-free only if every pair it touches has $\Ak = \Bk = 0$.

  Per-feature contribution against relative distance. Eight synthetic
  features; each panel plots $c_i(\delta)$ against $\delta$ with the feature's
  own mean as a dotted line. Every feature's curve changes sign over the range.
  The max/min ratio this figure was once described by is a grid-density
  artefact and is deliberately not plotted; see the remark below.%
}{fig:attrib}

Figure~\ref{fig:attrib} measures the effect directly, and the result is sharper
than a magnitude ratio. With $F = 8$ features on the $54$-point distance grid,
\textbf{every one of the eight contributions changes sign} somewhere in
$\delta \in [1, 8192]$. The magnitude of $|c_i|$ varies with a coefficient of
variation of $0.70 \pm 0.03$ across seeds, and the magnitudes at the two ends of
the range are, on average, the same size to within a factor of $10^{-0.08}$.
What varies is not how large the contribution is but which way it points.

This is the central result, and it is a stronger one than a large ratio would
have been. A feature whose attribution reverses sign cannot be summarised by any
position-independent number --- not approximately, but with the wrong sign. That
is what obstructs position-free attribution.

\begin{remark}[The ratio we previously led with was an artefact]
An earlier version reported a worst-feature ratio
$\max_\delta|c_i|/\min_\delta|c_i|$ of $920\times$. We withdrew it, and
Appendix~\ref{app:ratio} records the measurements that show why it measures the
sampling grid rather than the function. The replacements above are chosen for
stability: the coefficient of variation has $3.7\%$ seed variance and the
sign-crossing fraction has none. The artefact is pinned by
\texttt{rope\_attribution.statistics} and by the test suite, so it cannot be
quietly reinstated.
\end{remark}

The practical reading: an additive attribution with a position-free $A_{ij}$ is
not merely an approximation --- it is a mis-specification. It cannot represent
the measured curves for any constant, and the size of the failure is measured:
replacing $c_i(\delta)$ by its per-feature mean leaves a residual some $15$
orders of magnitude above the additivity residual, staying between $15.0$ and
$15.2$ as the grid is made $120\times$ denser. The correct object is per-feature
and per-distance (Section~\ref{sec:wellposed}); whether it is useful in practice
is a question about downstream circuits, which we do not measure.

\subsection{What the well-posed object is}
\label{sec:wellposed}
It is worth being concrete about the replacement, because ``do per-distance
attribution'' is otherwise an instruction without a definition. For a query
feature $i$ and a key feature $j$ at relative distance $\delta$, define the
$2\times 2$ coefficients
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    A_{ij}^{(k)}(\delta) \;=\;
    \cos\D\, A_{ij}^{A}[k] \;+\; \sin\D\, A_{ij}^{B}[k],\\
    \qquad k < \half ,
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where $A_{ij}^{A}[k]$ and $A_{ij}^{B}[k]$ are formed from
$A_{ij} = q_i^{\!\top}k_j$ by the same split-half grouping as
Equations~\ref{eq:A}--\ref{eq:B}. Then
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    A_{ij}(\delta) \;=\; \sum_{k < \half} A_{ij}^{(k)}(\delta)\\
    \;=\; \sum_{k < \half} R_k^{ij} \cos\bigl(\D - \psik^{ij}\bigr),
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:perpair}
\end{equation}
so the well-posed attribution object is not a $1\times1$ coefficient $A_{ij}$
but the $\delta$-indexed family
$\bigl\{A_{ij}^{(0)}(\delta), \dots, A_{ij}^{(\half-1)}(\delta)\bigr\}$,
together with the position-independent amplitudes $R_k^{ij}$. Equivalently, per
feature, $c_i(\delta) = \sum_j g_j A_{ij}(\delta)$ is an explicit finite sum of
$\half$ sinusoids in $\delta$ whose frequencies $\invf_k$ and amplitudes
$R_k^{ij}$ are fixed by the content.

Three properties follow without further measurement. It is \emph{finite}:
$\half$ terms, no summation over a learned dictionary. It is \emph{closed-form}:
no fit, no optimization, no forward pass, since $R_k^{ij}$ and $\psik^{ij}$ come
from $q_i$ and $k_j$ by Equations~\ref{eq:A}--\ref{eq:B}. And it is \emph{exact},
because Equation~\ref{eq:perpair} is an identity rather than an expansion. The
cost is that it is a function, not a number.

\subsection{What the function is worth}
\label{sec:worth}

An exact object that is expensive to use is not worth much, so the useful
question is not whether the decomposition is right --- it is, to the dtype floor
--- but what a position-free scalar costs. Here we price it.

For a rotary pair, the exact contribution over a distance range is
$A_k\cos(\delta\invf_k) + B_k\sin(\delta\invf_k)$, so the best possible
position-free scalar for that pair is the least-squares constant over the range,
namely $A_k \cdot \overline{\cos} + B_k \cdot \overline{\sin}$ with
$\overline{\cos}$ and $\overline{\sin}$ the range means of the cosine and sine.
Using the least-squares optimum rather than a convenient strawman is the point:
the number below is the cost of the \emph{best} position-free attribution, not
the cost of a careless one.

Across the three trained checkpoints of Section~\ref{sec:trained}, over every
rotary layer and head and on a $13$-point log-spaced grid from $1$ to $4096$,
that optimum reports the wrong sign on $27.0\%$ of per-pair, per-distance cells. Per model the rate is $28.4\%$ on
\texttt{pythia-160m}, $26.3\%$ on \texttt{llama-160m} and $26.4\%$ on
\texttt{SmolLM-135M}. Across all $558$ layer-heads the per-head rate never
leaves the band $21.3\%$ to $33.9\%$, so this is not one bad head but the
ordinary case. For a pair, relative error is the largest absolute deviation from
the exact per-distance value over the grid, divided by that pair's own peak
absolute contribution; the median is over pairs. The relative error is larger
than the signal itself: median $1.13$ against a peak of $1$.

The headline deserves three qualifications.
\emph{First}, the two summaries: $27.0\%$ is the equal-weight mean of the
three per-checkpoint rates; the mean over all $558$ heads is $26.90\%$
(\texttt{SmolLM-135M} contributes $270$ heads against $144$ each for the other
two). Computed before rounding, the two differ by $0.14$ points, about one
pooled interval half-width, and are not distinguishable at this resolution. In what
follows the headline is the equal-weight $27.0\%$, while the interval below
belongs to the head-pooled $26.90\%$.
\emph{Second}, the interval: resampling over the $558$ layer-heads --- over
\emph{heads}, not over the $2790$ head-prompt instances, since prompts repeat
weights and cells within a head share a query and key --- gives a $95\%$
percentile interval of $[0.2676, 0.2704]$ around $0.2690$, and the between-head
standard deviation of the per-head rates is $0.0170$. It is conditional on these
three checkpoints and says nothing about checkpoint or prompt selection.
\emph{Third}, the checkpoints do not all agree: a bootstrap over per-head rates
separates pythia from both others: pythia vs llama $+2.12$ points
$[+1.69, +2.55]$, pythia vs SmolLM $+2.01$ $[+1.58, +2.43]$. The llama--SmolLM
gap is $-0.12$ $[-0.26, +0.01]$, whose interval includes zero. The conclusion is
unchanged under three pre-registered seeds: the draw moves the rate by
$27.0421 \pm 0.0046\%$, below both.

Two further caveats. The rate is counted only over cells where the exact term
carries at least $5\%$ of its own peak; below that the exact value is
numerically indistinguishable from zero and ``sign'' is not meaningful. And the
statistic is mildly sensitive to grid density, since a coarser grid misses cells
near the zero crossings; Appendix~\ref{app:stats} gives the measured drift.

Beyond sign, the position-free ranking names the same leading pair as the exact
one on $49.4\%$--$60.1\%$ of triples, against $3.1\%$--$12.5\%$ for chance; the
per-head spread and the top-three rates are in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}.

\subsection{The same identities on trained weights}
\label{sec:trained}

Everything above is measured on synthetic vectors. That is a real limitation for
a claim about what trained models do, and it is also avoidable for the narrower
question of whether the algebra survives contact with real weights. We repeat the
central measurements on activations taken from three trained checkpoints, across
$5$ relative distances and every attention head of every rotary layer, with
$256$ random content pairs per head and prompt. The artifact records
$1.47$M--$11.06$M (layer, head, content-pair, rotary-pair) samples and the
$462{,}144$ degenerate \texttt{llama} pair-channel entries excluded.

Three findings, in decreasing order of how much they matter.

\textbf{The identities hold on trained weights, at the dtype floor.} The
relative-position property $\R_m^{\!\top}\R_n = \R_{n-m}$ is exact for real
projections, with a worst absolute error of $4.55\times10^{-13}$ across all three
checkpoints. The per-pair closed form of
Equation~\ref{eq:perpair} reproduces brute-force scores to a median relative
error of $6.68\times10^{-8}$ on \texttt{pythia-160m}, $3.57\times10^{-8}$ on
\texttt{llama-160m} and $5.85\times10^{-8}$ on \texttt{SmolLM-135M}, against a
float32 epsilon of $1.19\times10^{-7}$; bilinearity in content behaves the same
way at $7.70\times10^{-8}$, $5.99\times10^{-8}$ and $7.52\times10^{-8}$ --- each
a scaled residual, divided by the magnitude sum on which bilinearity is exact
rather than by the score, whose cancellation on a real head would dominate the
naive ratio. These are not numbers that happen to pass; they are what correct
arithmetic at float32 precision gives. A trained checkpoint does not introduce a
correction term to any of it.

\textbf{The obstruction of Section~\ref{sec:cond} is a fact about the weights.} The
share of an attention score taken by a feature's contribution is not a stable
quantity across heads. Pooling over heads, layers and prompts, the leading
feature's share ranges from $0.0181$ to $0.9999$ on \texttt{pythia-160m}, a
factor of $55.4$; on \texttt{llama-160m} it spans $0.0148$ to $0.9999$, a factor of $67.6$; on
\texttt{SmolLM-135M}, $0.0150$ to $0.9999$, a factor of $66.8$. The
interquartile range on \texttt{pythia-160m} alone runs from $0.186$ to $0.992$.
Some heads hand
almost the entire score to one feature; others spread it. Any per-feature
attribution method that reports one number per feature has to pick a convention
for that spread, and the convention will dominate the answer.

\textbf{Most pairs have a non-negligible position-carrying term.} We call a
rotary pair position-blind \emph{by convention} when the position-carrying share
of its amplitude, $|B_k|/R_k$, is below one percent (equivalently, $|B_k| <
0.01\,|A_k|$). This is a cutoff, not an algebraic fact: even when $B_k$ vanishes
it leaves $A_k\cos(\D)$, which still oscillates with distance. Measured on
trained weights, the fraction meeting the cutoff is $0.0054$ on
\texttt{pythia-160m}, $0.0065$ on \texttt{llama-160m} and $0.0063$ on
\texttt{SmolLM-135M}: between $0.54\%$ and $0.66\%$, within a factor of two
across three unrelated architectures. Treating the pair as a position-free
gate is therefore not a useful approximation on real weights: almost every pair
has a non-negligible phase offset.

One negative result: \texttt{gpt2} has no rotary embedding at all --- position
enters through a learned absolute table of size $1024$, so there is no
$\hat q/\hat k$ pair structure to attribute anything to. It is a useful control because it is the
model one reaches for first.

\subsection{The decomposition on a trained dictionary}
\label{sec:features}

The attribution above runs over random directions, which tests the algebra and
not the features. We therefore ran the per-distance decomposition on a public
dictionary: $32{,}768$ ReLU units trained with SAE Lens
\citep{bloom2024saelens} on \texttt{pythia-70m} \citep{biderman2023pythia},
layer $3$, which is the input to attention layer $4$. The dictionary acts
on the residual $x$ while attention sees $\mathrm{LN}(x)$, so a feature's share
is defined through the LayerNorm Jacobian at $x$: $h = J(x)x + c(x)$ is an
identity, not a linearisation, and writing
$x = b_{\mathrm{dec}} + \sum_i f_i W_{\mathrm{dec}}[i] + r$ splits $h$ over
features exactly, with $r$, $b_{\mathrm{dec}}$ and $c$ in an explicit base
bucket. On a $320$-token passage the bucket sum reproduces the score to a
relative $3.1\times10^{-14}$, and the residual fed to the dictionary is the
vector attention consumed to $2.7\times10^{-6}$, the float32 floor.

The result is two numbers that disagree, which is the point.

\emph{A table averaged over content.} At each of $14$ realized distances from
$1$ to $128$ we sample $24$ content pairs. The least-squares constant mis-signs
$4.7\%$ of material feature cells, or $10.0\%$ restricted to the rotary channels.
Neither is large: averaging over content at fixed distance removes most of the
oscillation before the constant is fitted.

\emph{A sweep with content fixed.} Holding each of $24$ pairs fixed and sweeping
$\delta$ over the paper's $48$-point grid from $1$ to $8192$ is the measurement
comparable to the synthetic $27.0\%$. The total per-feature profile mis-signs
$0.03\%$ of material cells; the rotary-only profile mis-signs $22.4\%$, between
$19.5\%$ and $27.9\%$ per head, and feature pairs $10.5\%$.

The gap is the structural claim of Section~\ref{sec:partial}, measured. This head
rotates $16$ of its $64$ channels; the other $48$ contribute a term that no
distance changes, and here it dominates. A position-free table fitted to the
total therefore looks almost perfect --- about the part of the score RoPE does
not touch. In the subspace RoPE acts on, the same estimator is wrong about one
cell in five, at very nearly the synthetic rate. A reader of a total table would
see $0.03\%$ and conclude that position does not matter at this layer; the
rotary subspace says otherwise (Figure~\ref{fig:qkcase}).

The attribution is not concentrated: the base bucket carries $1.46\times$ the
score magnitude on average and the top-$32$ features $3.71\times$, above one
because the terms cancel. The decomposition is exact but
not sparse in its effect. One layer of one $70$M-parameter checkpoint is an
existence proof about real features, not a population estimate.

\subsection{What context extension fixes, and what it does not}
\label{sec:context}

Appendix~\ref{app:figs:context} compares the four schemes on the $5$-point grid and
carries the derivation; three findings survive compression.
\emph{The worst angle is untouched.} $\max_k|\D|$ is equal for RoPE and \yarn\
at every distance tested --- exactly $4096\rad$ at $\delta = 4096$ --- because
\yarn's ramp leaves the fastest channel at its original inverse frequency, so it
still contributes exactly $1\rad$ per token. \emph{The median falls by a fixed
factor}: $0.4464\times$ at every distance, which lifts the fraction of channels
admitting the quadratic linearization from $0.0625$ to $0.3438$ at
$\delta = 512$, and from $0.0000$ to $0.2188$ at $\delta = 4096$. \emph{Neither
rescues a global linearization}: the amplitude-weighted error at $\delta = 8192$
is $1.24572\times10^{6}$ for RoPE against $1.24179\times10^{6}$ for \yarn, a
ratio of $0.996840$, because the error is carried by the same fast channels
\yarn\ does not touch. Position interpolation reaches $1225.06$ there precisely
by shrinking the fast channel into a regime a trained model does not natively
use; we do not measure that trade-off.

\begin{remark}
Context extension changes \emph{which} channels admit a linearization and
\emph{when}, but not the fact that the worst channel is out of range --- and no
amount of angle damping rescues position-free attribution, whose failure is in
R2.
\end{remark}

Two further findings are in the appendices, neither part of R1--R3:
Appendix~\ref{sec:partial} (partial RoPE yields an \emph{exactly} position-free
sub-score) and Appendix~\ref{sec:mscale} (\yarn's magnitude factor acts as a
temperature, monotone across $18$ scales in entropy).

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{No downstream task, and one dictionary.} Most of the algebraic claims
rest on synthetic vectors with fixed seeds. The $8$ directions our attribution
runs over are random, and the synthetic result is a property of random directions
in a $64$-dimensional head, not of real feature geometry. Section~\ref{sec:trained} uses the three checkpoints and their
content-pair draws at $135$--$160$M parameters, and Section~\ref{sec:features}
runs the attribution on one public dictionary. Section~\ref{sec:trained}
confirms the identities on their activations; the checkpoints are measurement
instruments, but are not evaluated on a task. Neither connects to a task: no
retrieval benchmark, no language-modelling evaluation, no causal patching. One
layer of one checkpoint is a description of that layer --- none of its content is
evaluated on a downstream task. One passage and $24$ content pairs per head is
an existence proof about real features, not a population estimate.

\textbf{Single synthetic configuration.} The main synthetic experiments use
$d = 64$, $\mathrm{base} = 10000$, $s = 32$, pre-extension context $2048$,
and one query/key draw per experiment. The robustness sweep in
Appendix~\ref{app:provenance} varies the base and the head dimension, and
Section~\ref{sec:trained} uses three checkpoints at $135$--$160$M parameters;
neither resolves how the dominant channels depend on $d$ through $\half$, and we
do not claim that dependence.

\textbf{Approximation choices.} The linearization threshold is not swept;
Appendix~\ref{app:scope} records what a different truncation would move.

\textbf{Scope of the exactness claim.} Exactness removes one objection to
RoPE-based attribution, not all of them; Appendix~\ref{app:scope} states the
boundary.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Appendix~\ref{app:related} gives the full positioning. No positional extension
keeps the fastest channel within $0.1\rad$ for more than three tokens; the
per-pair algebra is known
\citep{chachamovits2026phase,liang2026roaware}; the QK-attribution method paper
states that RoPE breaks its premise and defers it \citep{kamath2025tracing}; the
closest feature-level decomposition has no positional term
\citep{lee2026contrastive}. Concurrent work separates position from features for
faithfulness \citep{kim2026pax}, and a submission under review finds that RoPE
surrogates chosen by native fit misrank under positional intervention (OpenReview
\texttt{Eu2I5jI8Cs}), so ``position-free attribution is lossy'' is no longer ours
to claim. What is missing is the per-feature, per-distance table and the sign
reversal within it, which we measure---including on a trained dictionary
(Section~\ref{sec:features}).

\section{Conclusion}

RoPE's effect on feature-level attention attribution is exactly characterizable
in the relative frame: the score is $q^{\!\top}\R_{\delta}k$, and each of the
$\half$ rotary pairs contributes a sinusoid with a position-independent amplitude
and a linear phase. Score attribution is exact for a fixed position pair, to
$3.9\times10^{-14}$, the query and key magnitudes are exactly
position-independent, and position-conditionality rather than bilinearity is the
obstruction: a feature's contribution reverses sign across distance while the
total stays additive to $5.0\times10^{-14}$. Context extension changes which
channels can be linearized without touching the worst one: \yarn\ buys a
larger linearizable majority at an amplitude-weighted error ratio of only
$0.996840$, not a safe global linearization. Partial RoPE is the clean
exception: its unrotated sub-score is exactly position-free, and the rotated
block remains orthogonal and preserves the norm exactly.
On a trained dictionary the rotary subspace reproduces the synthetic
sign-reversal rate while the total profile hides it. There is no downstream
task, no retrieval benchmark, no accuracy figure; one dictionary at one layer is
an existence proof, not a population claim. Appendix~\ref{app:note} corrects
two informal statements.

\subsection{The three results are not independent}
\label{sec:synthesis}

It is worth stating explicitly how R1, R2 and R3 relate, because they are
usually discussed as three separate observations and they are not.

R1 and R2 pull in opposite directions and both are correct. The additive
decomposition of Equation~\ref{eq:additive} is exact to
$5.0\times10^{-14}$ --- the coefficients $A_{ij}$ exist and are real numbers,
not fudges. But those real numbers are functions of $\delta$: each rotary pair
contributes $\Rk\cos(\D + \psik)$, which changes sign twice per period. Exactness
therefore buys nothing
against position-conditionality: the map from features to score is exactly
additive at every fixed distance and wildly non-constant across distances. This
is the sense in which position-conditionality, not bilinearity, is the operative
obstruction, and it is what separates R1 from R2: an attribution scheme that
computes $A_{ij}$ correctly at $\delta = 8$ and reuses it at $\delta = 512$ has
made an error far larger than any it would make by ignoring RoPE altogether.

R3 is orthogonal to both, and this is the part most likely to be
misunderstood. The linearization failure is a statement about the
\emph{approximation} $\cos \D \mapsto 1 - \D^2/2$, not about attribution. One
could perfectly well attribute a RoPE head exactly --- that is R1 --- and still
be unable to linearize it. Conversely, one could linearize a head perfectly and
still have no position-free attribution, which is the situation under full
\rope\ at any $\delta > 3$. So the two literatures --- ``we need a wider
context'' and ``we need position-free feature attribution'' --- are addressing
different problems, and a method that answers one does not answer the other.
What \yarn\ does is extend the distance range over which a majority of channels
can be linearized, from $0/32$ to $11/32$ at $\delta \approx 861$. What it does
not do is make the worst channel linearizable, and the worst channel is what
determines whether the head as a whole can be treated as linear.

We mention this because the natural reading of Table~\ref{tab:spectrum} is
that \yarn\ is ``better'', and it is better on one axis and indifferent on the
other. The honest summary is the one in the remark above: context extension
changes which channels admit a linearization and when, and does not change the
fact that the worst channel is out of range.

\section{Method}
\label{app:method}

All measurements come from a from-scratch float64 NumPy implementation,
regenerated by a single entry point and written to
\texttt{results/measurements.json}. We use $d = 64$, $\mathrm{base} = 10000$,
extension factor $s = 32$, and pre-extension context $2048$, unless a table
says otherwise. Two grids are used in the structural and scheme-comparison
tables and are kept distinct throughout: a
$5$-point grid $\{1, 64, 512, 2048, 4096\}$ for the structural and
scheme-comparison tables, and a $54$-point log-spaced integer grid from $1$ to
$8192$ for the figures. Every quantity carries its grid with it, so numbers
drawn from different grids are never compared as though they were the same.

\paragraph{Measured quantities.}
\begin{itemize}
  \itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Structural residuals.} Max absolute error of the
    relative-position property over $42$ position pairs; max absolute error of
    $\bigl\|\R_m q\bigr\| - \bigl\|q\bigr\|$ and
    $\bigl\|\R_n k\bigr\| - \bigl\|k\bigr\|$; max relative error of bilinearity
    over $35$ score checks; max absolute error of the per-pair closed form
    against brute force.
  \item \textbf{Feature additivity.} We build $q = \sum_i f_i d_i^{(q)}$ and
    $k = \sum_j g_j d_j^{(k)}$ over $F = 8$ synthetic directions with
    non-negative coefficients, project each summand independently, and check
    $s(\delta) \stackrel{?}{=} \sum_{ij} A_{ij}(\delta)$ where
    $A_{ij} = q_i^{\!\top}\R_{\delta}k_j$.
  \item \textbf{Position-conditionality.} For each feature $i$ we record the
    signed curve $c_i(\delta)$ over the whole range, and report three summaries of
    it: whether it changes sign at all, its coefficient of variation in
    magnitude, and the residual left by replacing it with a position-independent
    constant. We deliberately do \emph{not} report
    $\max_\delta |c_i| / \min_\delta |c_i|$: that is a property of the sampling
    grid, and Section~\ref{sec:cond} explains what went wrong when we did.
  \item \textbf{Linearization.} We replace $\cos \D$ by $1 - \D^2/2$ and
    $\sin \D$ by $\D$, and measure the damage per pair, normalized by that
    pair's own amplitude $\Rk$, plus the amplitude-weighted total. The per-pair
    normalization is not a detail. The exact score is a sum of $\half$
    sinusoids in $\delta$ that cancel; as a function of $\delta$ it passes
    through zero repeatedly, so a total-score relative error
    $\bigl|\hat s - s\bigr| / \bigl|s\bigr|$ diverges at those zeros for
    reasons that have nothing to do with the quality of the approximation. Any
    scheme that reports a total-score relative error under a linearization is,
    at the least, reporting something dominated by cancellation. Normalizing by
    $\Rk$ makes the error dimensionless and comparable across pairs: it is $0$
    when the pair is exactly linear and $1$ when the error is as large as the
    term being approximated. It is a scale, not a bound: Section~\ref{sec:context}
    shows the fastest pair's normalized error passing the size of its own
    contribution within a few tokens.
  \item \textbf{A ``linearizable'' channel} is one with $|\D| \le 0.1\rad$
    throughout the grid. This is a definition, a dimensionful threshold, not a
    measurement.
  \item \textbf{Entropy.} Attention entropy $H/\ln T$ for one query against
    $T = 256$ keys, with and without $\mathrm{mscale}$, over $18$ extension
    factors.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Every figure ships the array it plotted as a CSV
sidecar, so no claim in this paper is read off a pixel, and the appendix records
which file and key every quoted number came from. The audit is mechanical: each
literal in the tables is either an entry of \texttt{results/measurements.json},
an entry of a figure CSV, or a stated quotient of two such entries.

\subsection{Partial RoPE gives an exactly position-free sub-score}
\label{sec:partial}
\pip\ rotates only a fraction $p = n_{\mathrm{rot}}/d$ of channels and leaves
the rest untouched. The score then splits into an unrotated part and a rotated
part, and the unrotated part is position-free by construction --- not
approximately, but exactly, because no rotation is applied to it.

\paperfigure{fig07_partial_rope}{%
  Partial RoPE at $p = 0.25$: the rotated and unrotated sub-scores against
  relative distance (left), and each sub-score's deviation from its own mean
  (right). The clean sub-score is flat to the last bit; the rotated one
  oscillates. See Section~\ref{sec:partial}.%
}{fig:partial}

Figure~\ref{fig:partial} shows the split. At $d = 64$ with
$n_{\mathrm{rot}} = 16$: the unrotated sub-score's spread over the $5$-point
grid is \emph{exactly} $0.0$, and over the finer $54$-point grid it
is also exactly $0.0$ --- the plotted constant is $0.6857345646988313$ at every
distance. The rotated sub-score, over those same grids, varies by $20.65$ and
$24.36$ respectively. The clean part carries a mean magnitude share of the
score of $0.076$ (JSON grid) and $0.231$ (figure grid): a genuinely
position-free bilinear term, though a smaller part of the score than the
rotated one at these distances.

This is the one setting in which a position-free additive attribution is exactly
correct, and it is correct because three quarters of the channels carry no
position at all. That makes \pip\ the natural recommendation for feature-level
attribution in a model that has it.

\begin{remark}[$\pip$ preserves the norm too]
There is a subtlety here that a natural implementation gets wrong. Partial
rotary is orthogonal, and so preserves the norm exactly, as full RoPE does: we
measure a deviation below the float64 noise floor at
$n_{\mathrm{rot}} = 16$, the same as full RoPE. This holds only if the rotated
block is paired \emph{within itself} --- channel $i$ with channel
$i + n_{\mathrm{rot}}/2$, the GPT-NeoX layout used by models such as
\texttt{EleutherAI/pythia-160m}. Pairing $i$ with $i + d/2$ across the whole head
instead leaves every touched pair half rotated and half not; that variant is not
orthogonal and is not what any published model does. We mention it because the
failure is silent: the code runs, the numbers look reasonable, and only a
norm check reveals the difference. The one claim we withdraw here is any
suggestion that partial rotation damages the magnitude gate.
\end{remark}

\subsection{$\mathrm{mscale}$ is a temperature}
\label{sec:mscale}

\yarn's magnitude factor of Equation~\ref{eq:mscale} scales the $\cos$ and
$\sin$ tables, which RoPE applies to both the query and the key, so every logit
carries $\mathrm{mscale}^2$ --- precisely a temperature on the attention
distribution. At $s = 32$ we measure $\mathrm{mscale} = 1.3465736$, matching the
closed form $0.1\ln 32 + 1$ to all printed digits.

The temperature reading is not ours alone. Attention-entropy stability is
already the stated design target of logit-scaling context extensions
\citep{zhang2024hundredsamples}, and the theory of inverse-temperature
scaling in self-attention \citep{hayase2026criticaltemperature} proves the
scale past which the attention entropy collapses. What is new here is the
source of the temperature: not prescribed by us but inherited, YaRN's
magnitude factor squared by the split-half rotation into the logit, and
its consequence measured across the extension range the other works
optimize for.

\paperfigure{fig08_mscale_entropy}{%
  Normalized attention entropy $H/\ln T$ against extension factor, with and
  without $\mathrm{mscale}$ (left), and the measured $\mathrm{mscale}$ against
  its closed form (right). $\mathrm{mscale}$ sharpens the attention
  distribution. See Section~\ref{sec:mscale}.%
}{fig:entropy}

Figure~\ref{fig:entropy} plots this. With one query against $256$ random keys,
$\mathrm{mscale}$ moves the normalized entropy from $H/\ln T = 0.9233$ to
$0.7687$, a drop of $0.1546$; the raw entropy
falls from $5.1198$ to $4.2627$ nats. Across the full range of $18$ extension
factors from $1$ to $128$, the effect is monotone: $H/\ln T$ falls to $0.6792$
as $\mathrm{mscale}$ rises to $1.4852$. Lowering entropy is a sharpening of the
attention distribution: fewer keys receive appreciable mass. This is a real,
measurable, and easily overlooked side effect of context extension that is not
part of its frequency story, and it is independent of everything in
Sections~\ref{sec:exact}--\ref{sec:context}. We report it as a measured
consequence and make no claim about whether it helps or hurts a trained model,
which we do not measure.

\section{Context extension: the derivations}
\label{app:context}

This appendix carries what the main text summarises: the full per-channel
description of what \yarn\ does, the frequency ladder it produces, and the
algebra showing why raising $\theta_0$ is not the same scheme as dividing
positions. Section~\ref{sec:context} reports the measurements.


\yarn\ \citep{peng2023yarn} extends context by leaving $\mathrm{base}$ alone and,
per Equation~\ref{eq:mscale}, modifying the inverse frequencies per channel: short-wavelength (high-frequency)
channels keep their $\invf$, long-wavelength (low-frequency) channels are
divided by the extension factor $s$, and the two are blended linearly in
between. It then multiplies all cosines and sines by a magnitude factor
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{mscale} = 0.1 \ln s + 1 .
  \label{eq:mscale}
\end{equation}
Note what is \emph{not} here: $\mathrm{base}$ is unchanged, and the fastest
channel is explicitly left alone. This is the source of R3.

\paperfigure{fig01_frequency_ladder}{%
  Inverse frequency against rotary-pair index for the four schemes. RoPE is
  the reference ladder; position interpolation divides every entry by $32$;
  \yarn\ leaves the fastest $9$ of $32$ entries bit-for-bit identical to RoPE
  and ramps only the slow tail down by exactly $32\times$; the
  ``$\mathrm{base}=500000$'' ladder is a uniform reshaping. See
  Section~\ref{sec:context}.%
}{app:fig:ladder}

The frequency ladder of Figure~\ref{app:fig:ladder} makes the asymmetry explicit.
Under \yarn\ at $s = 32$, the fastest $9$ of the $32$ rotary pairs have inverse
frequencies \emph{bit-for-bit identical} to plain RoPE, and the slowest pair is
slowed by exactly a factor of $32.00$. The scheme is thus not a uniform
rescaling at all: it is a ramp that leaves $9$ of $32$ entries untouched.
Position interpolation, by contrast, differs from RoPE in all $32$ entries.

Position interpolation \citep{chen2023positionalinterp} instead divides
positions by $s$ uniformly. It is worth being precise that this is
\emph{not} the same scheme as raising $\mathrm{base}$. Dividing positions
divides every entry by $s$, whereas raising $\mathrm{base}$ to
$\mathrm{base}\cdot s$ multiplies entry $k$ by $s^{-2k/d}$:
\[
  \mathrm{inv\_freq}_k \;\mapsto\; \mathrm{base}^{-2k/d}\,s^{-1}
  \qquad\text{versus}\qquad
  \mathrm{base}^{-2k/d}\,s^{-2k/d}.
\]
The two agree only where $2k/d = 1$, that is at $k = d/2$, which lies outside
the ladder $k = 0,\dots,d/2-1$. They therefore never coincide on any channel.
The sharpest instance is the fastest channel: $\theta_0 = \mathrm{base}^{0} = 1$
for \emph{every} base, so raising the base from $10000$ to $500000$ leaves
$\theta_0$, and hence $\max_k |D_k|$, completely unchanged, while dividing
positions by $s$ divides it. This is exactly what our table shows: at
$\delta = 512$ position interpolation reaches $\max_k|D_k| = 16$ and the
$\mathrm{base} = 500000$ ladder still sits at $512$. We nonetheless measure all
four as separate rows, because they are frequently conflated.

All measurements come from a from-scratch float64 NumPy implementation, at
$d = 64$, $\mathrm{base} = 10000$, extension factor $s = 32$, and pre-extension
context $2048$, unless a table says otherwise. Two grids are used and are kept
distinct throughout: a $5$-point grid for the structural and scheme-comparison
tables, and a $54$-point log-spaced grid from $1$ to $8192$ for the figures. No
claim is read off a pixel, and Appendix~\ref{app:provenance} records which file
and key every quoted number came from; the full protocol is in
Appendix~\ref{app:method}.

\section{Setup: definitions, derivations and proofs}
\label{app:setup}

The main text states the three propositions the results rest on. This appendix
carries what they are derived from: the frequency ladder, the relative-frame
factorisation, the per-pair closed form, and the proofs themselves.

\subsection{Rotary position embeddings}

Let $d$ be an even head dimension and let $x_m \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the
pre-projection content at absolute position $m$. RoPE defines
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \invf_k &= \mathrm{base}^{-2k/d},\; k = 0, \dots, \half - 1,\\
    \mathrm{base} &= 10000 .
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
and applies a block-diagonal rotation $\R_m$ whose $k$-th $2\times2$ block
rotates by the angle $m \cdot \invf_k$. With $q = W_Q x_m$ and $k = W_K x_n$ the
projections taken before rotation, the score at query position $m$ and key
position $n$ is
\begin{equation}
  s(m,n) \;=\; \bigl(\R_m q\bigr)^{\!\top} \bigl(\R_n k\bigr).
  \label{eq:rope}
\end{equation}
This is the formulation of \citet{su2021ropeformer}. Our implementation follows
the split-half convention, pairing channel $i$ with $i + \half$, so that one
frequency vector of length $\half$ drives all $d$ channels.

\subsection{The relative frame}

The rotation matrices $\R_m$ are orthogonal, hence $\R_m^{\!\top}\R_m = I$.
Factoring through the relative frame gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \R_m^{\!\top} \bigl(\R_n k\bigr) \;=\; \R_m^{\!\top}\R_n k \;=\; \R_{n-m} k,\\
    \R_m^{\!\top} \bigl(\R_m q\bigr) \;=\; q .
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
  s(m,n) \;=\; q^{\!\top} \R_{\delta} k, \qquad \delta = n - m,
\end{equation}
which depends on the two absolute positions only through their difference,
reducing the score of Equation~\ref{eq:rope} to a function of one number. This is
the relative-position property, and it is what makes the rest of the paper
possible: from here on, ``position'' means $\delta$ and nothing else.

\begin{proposition}[The gate is position-free]
\label{prop:gate}
$\bigl|\R_{\delta} k\bigr| = \bigl|k\bigr|$ for all $\delta$. Consequently the
magnitude of the query and of the key is exactly position-independent, and all
position information in $s(\delta)$ is carried by the rotation angle alone.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:gate}]
$\R_\delta$ is block diagonal with $2\times 2$ rotation blocks
$\R_\delta^{(k)}$ of Equation~\ref{eq:pair}, and each block is orthogonal since
its columns are orthonormal: $(\cos\D)^2 + (\sin\D)^2 = 1$. Hence
$\bigl|\R_\delta k\bigr|^2 = k^{\!\top}\R_\delta^{\!\top}\R_\delta k =
k^{\!\top}k = \bigl|k\bigr|^2$, and taking the positive square root gives the
claim. Both statements hold for every $\delta$, so the norms carry no positional
information while the angle $\D$ is where all of it enters.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:gate} is the load-bearing statement for norm preservation:
the rotation changes the angle but not the magnitude. The per-pair closed form
below supplies the complementary content/position split used in sparse-feature
interpretability: $\Rk$ and $\psik$ are functions of the content alone, while
only $\D$ depends on distance. We measure the numerical residual of the
orthogonality in Section~\ref{sec:exact}.

\subsection{The per-pair closed form}
\label{sec:pair}

Group the head into rotary pairs $(k, k + \half)$, so that $\R_{\delta}$ mixes
exactly these two channels. In the split-half convention, the $k$-th block of
$\R_{\delta}$ acts on $(q[k], q[k+\half])$ and $(k[k], k[k+\half])$ as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \R_\delta^{(k)} \;=\;
    \begin{pmatrix} \cos \D & -\sin \D \\ \sin \D & \cos \D \end{pmatrix},
    \\[-1pt]
    \qquad \D = \delta \invf_k .
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}
with the same $\D$ on both diagonals. Expanding $q^{\!\top}\R_{\delta}k$ over
that block and collecting terms gives, exactly,
\begin{align}
  \Ak &= q[k]\,k[k] \;+\; q[k+\half]\,k[k+\half] \label{eq:A}\\
  \Bk &= q[k+\half]\,k[k] \;-\; q[k]\,k[k+\half] \label{eq:B}\\
  c_k(\delta) &= \Ak \cos\D + \Bk \sin\D \\
  &= \Rk\cos(\D - \psik), \\
  \Rk &= \operatorname{hypot}(\Ak, \Bk), \\
  \psik &= \operatorname{atan2}(\Bk, \Ak).
\end{align}
and the score is $s(\delta) = \sum_{k < \half} c_k(\delta)$. The two
coefficients are therefore fixed by the content vectors alone, and the whole of
the $\delta$-dependence sits in the single angle $\D$.

A concrete instance makes this visible. The amplitude $\Rk$ of the fastest rotary
pair, $k = 0$, takes the single value $0.9526294617807264$ at every one of the
$54$ distances and under both \rope\ and \yarn\ --- one value, not fifty-four. A
mid-frequency pair, $k = 12$, has amplitude $2.3046990966686227$ under the same
key and query, so amplitudes are not interchangeable across pairs either; each
pair has its own gate, and each gate is distance-free.

Note also what the closed form does \emph{not} depend on. $\Rk$ is a
hypot of two products of content coordinates; it is the same number at
$\delta = 1$ and at $\delta = 8192$. That is the content of
Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}, and it is what lets us speak of a per-pair
``gate'' at all. Two structural facts follow immediately, and both are exact
rather than asymptotic.

\begin{proposition}[Amplitude and phase are content-determined]
\label{prop:blind}
The coefficients $\Ak$ and $\Bk$ of Equations~\ref{eq:A}--\ref{eq:B}, and
therefore $\Rk$ and $\psik$, are functions of the content vectors alone: none of
them depends on $\delta$. The whole of the $\delta$-dependence of $c_k$ lies in the
single angle $\D = \delta\invf_k$. Moreover $c_k$ is a \emph{non-constant}
sinusoid in $\delta$ whenever $\Rk > 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}]
Read $\Ak$ and $\Bk$ off Equations~\ref{eq:A}--\ref{eq:B}: each is a sum or
difference of products $q[\cdot]k[\cdot]$ of content coordinates, and neither
$\delta$ nor $\invf_k$ appears. Both are therefore $\delta$-independent, and so
are $\Rk = \operatorname{hypot}(\Ak, \Bk)$ and
$\psik = \operatorname{atan2}(\Bk, \Ak)$, functions of $(\Ak, \Bk)$ alone.

For non-constancy, substitute the closed form $c_k(\delta) = \Rk\cos(\D - \psik)$.
Since $\invf_k > 0$ for every $k < \half$, $\delta$ ranging over any interval
longer than $\pi/\invf_k$ sweeps the argument through an arc of more than $\pi$,
and $\cos$ is not constant on such an arc.

We state no dichotomy about ``position-blindness'' here, because none exists.
In particular $\Bk = 0$ is \emph{not} a blind case: it gives $\psik = 0$ and
leaves $c_k(\delta) = \Ak\cos\D$, a full oscillation rather than a constant.
The threshold notion used in Section~\ref{sec:trained} is a separate,
approximate convention with a stated cutoff, not a consequence of this
proposition.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Conditionality]
\label{prop:cond}
Let $I$ index the rotary pairs a feature touches, and suppose the frequencies
$\invf_k$ are distinct and positive over $k \in I$. Then the feature's
contribution is independent of $\delta$ if and only if $\Ak = \Bk = 0$ for every
$k \in I$ --- that is, if and only if the feature contributes nothing at all.
There is no intermediate case in which the contribution is position-free.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:cond}]
The contribution is the trigonometric polynomial
\[
  g(\delta) \;=\; \sum_{k \in I} \bigl(\Ak\cos(\delta\invf_k)
    + \Bk\sin(\delta\invf_k)\bigr)
\]
over the distinct positive frequencies $\{\invf_k : k \in I\}$. The functions
$\{\cos(\alpha\delta), \sin(\alpha\delta)\}_{\alpha \in S}$ for any finite set $S$
of distinct positive frequencies are linearly independent on every interval of
positive length: a combination vanishing on such an interval is a trigonometric
polynomial vanishing on an open set, so the uniqueness theorem forces every
coefficient to zero. Applying this with $\alpha = \invf_k$ gives
$g \equiv 0 \Rightarrow \Ak = \Bk = 0$ for all $k \in I$, and any
$\delta$-independent $g$ is a constant. In particular no proper subset of the
touched pairs can be the position-free one: a single pair with $\Ak \ne 0$ already
makes the sum non-constant.

When $g$ is not identically zero it is a non-constant trigonometric polynomial, so
it takes both signs once the window is long enough relative to the smallest
$\invf_k$. That is the sign reversal we measure in Section~\ref{sec:cond} rather
than assume.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:cond} is the whole of R2, stated before any measurement.
Section~\ref{sec:cond} measures how large the effect is.

\subsection{Context extension, and what \yarn\ actually does}

\yarn\ \citep{peng2023yarn} leaves $\mathrm{base}$ alone and modifies the
inverse frequencies per channel: high-frequency channels keep their $\invf$,
low-frequency channels are divided by the extension factor $s$, and the two
are blended linearly in between, with every cosine and sine then multiplied by
$\mathrm{mscale} = 0.1\ln s + 1$ (Equation~\ref{eq:mscale}). What is
\emph{not} here matters most: the fastest channel is explicitly left
untouched, and that is the mechanism behind R3.

Two facts from the derivation are worth stating before the measurements.
Under \yarn\ at $s = 32$ the fastest $9$ of the $32$ rotary pairs have inverse
frequencies \emph{bit-for-bit identical} to plain RoPE, so the scheme is a
ramp over the slow tail rather than a uniform rescaling --- and position
interpolation \citep{chen2023positionalinterp}, which divides every position
by $s$, differs from RoPE in all $32$ entries. Raising $
\mathrm{base}$ is a third scheme again: it multiplies entry $k$ by
$s^{-2k/d}$ rather than by $s^{-1}$, so it leaves $\theta_0 = 1$ and hence
$\max_k |D_k|$ unchanged, while dividing positions divides it. The three are
routinely conflated, so all four are measured as separate rows.
The full derivation, the frequency ladder of
Figure~\ref{app:fig:ladder}, and the algebra of why they never coincide on
any channel are in Appendix~\ref{app:context}.

\section{Related work, in full}
\label{app:related}

\textbf{Position encodings and length extrapolation.} RoPE
\citep{su2021ropeformer} is the scheme studied here. \alpibib\ 
\citep{press2021train} removes position from the attention pattern by a linear
bias, so it has no per-channel angle spectrum and no version of the
linearization problem of Section~\ref{sec:context}; GaLA
\citep{loveland2026graph} adds a bias instead, undoing RoPE's
bandwidth-dependent attention decay on graph-linearized inputs at inference
time --- again an attention-pattern fix, not a per-feature decomposition. Among extension schemes,
position interpolation \citep{chen2023positionalinterp} rescales positions
uniformly and is the most favourable on linearizable fraction in our table;
\yarn\ \citep{peng2023yarn} keeps the base and ramps per frequency, which is
what makes $\max_k|\D|$ invariant under it. \lengthxtrap\ 
\citep{sun2022lengthextrap} covers the partial case of Section~\ref{sec:partial},
\longrope\ \citep{ding2024longrope} per-channel non-uniform extension,
and \ropetheory\ \citep{barbero2024roundandround} RoPE's frequency
content from an optimization perspective. The ``raise $\mathrm{base}$ from
$10000$ to $500000$'' recipe is a fourth scheme: it multiplies channel $k$ by
$s^{-2k/d}$ rather than by $s^{-1}$, so it leaves $\theta_0 = 1$ and hence
$\max_k|\D|$ untouched. We measure it separately for that reason.

A fifth construction, DaRoPE \citep{levy2026darope}, keeps standard RoPE on the
fast bands and replaces absolute position on the slow bands with bounded
coordinates learned from contextual representations, motivated by slow-band
wavelengths that exceed the training context and expose models to unseen
angles. It is data-aware rather than distance-only --- an axis this paper does
not vary --- and it leaves the fastest channel, whose linearization is measured
to fail in Section~\ref{sec:context}, exactly as plain RoPE has it.

A sixth construction diagnoses rather than extends the encoding. PoPE
\citep{gopalakrishnan2026pope} argues that RoPE entangles the content factor
(the what) with the relative-position factor (the where): in polar form each
channel contributes $|q_c||k_c|\cos(\D\theta_c + \alpha_c - \beta_c)$, where
$\alpha_c$ and $\beta_c$ are the query's and key's own phases, so content
rotates the offset at which a channel matches and a head cannot express an
independent content preference and an independent position preference. Their
Indirect Indexing diagnostic isolates the two factors, and their fix ---
replacing the interaction term with a per-frequency learnable offset inside
the cosine --- improves held-out perplexity on OpenWebText at every model
size they test and extrapolates zero-shot beyond the training
length where \yarn\ requires fine-tuning. The fix is architectural; the
measurement here is of the embedding as it stands. For fixed $q,k$ the score
is exactly a function of $\D$ (Section~\ref{sec:exact}), and what a
position-free summary of it must carry is the per-pair sign structure
measured in Section~\ref{sec:trained}: the coupling PoPE removes by
construction is, from this side, the structure an attribution has to
represent.

\textbf{Feature-level attribution.} The additive decomposition of activations
into learned features originates in the superposition line
\citep{elhage2022toymodels}, with sparse autoencoders as an operational extractor
\citep{cunningham2023sparseautoencoders}. Attribution on attention heads runs
from attribution patching \citep{syed2023attributionpatching} to the tuned lens
\citep{belrose2023tunedlens}, sparse feature circuits
\citep{marks2024sparsefeaturecircuits}, transcoder-based discovery
\citep{dunefsky2024transcoders} and RoPE-LIME
\citep{picov2026ropelime}, which uses the rotary embedding only as a
locality kernel for input perturbations on closed models. All of these methods
attribute within a given forward pass, and none varies the distance between
two positions; the positional encoding is part of the forward pass, not the
attribution target. The nearest work from the other side, Bakish and
colleagues \citep{bakish2025positionallrp}, propagates relevance through
Rotary, Learnable and Absolute encodings --- there the encoding is a
recipient of relevance, not the object whose per-distance score function
is measured. Exactness itself has a neighbor: exact finite attention
responses \citep{huang2026exactfinite} predict how a positional attention
edit responds, from the RoPE derivative $\partial_p z(p) = A z(p)$ and its
integral, carried through the softmax without linearising either rotation
or normalisation. The response to an intervention and the score function
itself are different objects; this paper measures the latter, at every
relative distance, in closed form.

\textbf{The algebra is not new, and we do not claim it is.} Chachamovits
\citep{chachamovits2026phase} writes a rotary head as a sum of per-pair terms
$\rho\cos(\alpha - \beta + (m-n)\theta)$ with $\rho = |q_j||k_j|$ ---
our closed form --- and remarks that the term goes negative near $\pi$; the
same identity appears in KV-cache quantization work \citep{liang2026roaware}, and
the per-frequency spectral structure has been measured on pretrained
checkpoints in weight space \citep{li2026fingerprint}. What is claimed here is
the attribution semantics and its consequence, not the identity. Nor is
the bilinear form innocent: propagation rules for attention built on it
violate the implementation-invariance axiom \citep{you2025lrploo}. The
decomposition here is an identity rather than a rule, and the cost of
its sign structure is measured rather than assumed away.

\textbf{The sign reversal appears unreported.} Our searches returned no paper
reporting that a feature's contribution to a rotary score changes sign as the
relative distance grows. The closest is Jedryszek and Crook
\citep{jedryszek2026retrieval}, who attribute per frequency pair with verified
additivity and then \emph{exclude} sign-inconsistent bands as numerically
unreliable. We read that as our phenomenon being discarded as noise; on the
decompositions we test it is exact and its magnitude is the feature's own
amplitude. Reporting it as something to exclude is not the same as measuring it
across a range and showing that no position-free summary can represent it.

\textbf{What the sweep found next to our claim.} Wu and colleagues
\citep{wu2026endofrope} derive a context-length bound beyond which a fixed
attention-score comparison cannot jointly avoid \emph{semantic reversal} and
positional insensitivity: a head-level comparison failing at long context,
whereas ours is per-feature, per-distance, and holds at short distances too.
Jonasson \citep{jonasson2025rotaryoffset} measures the norms and angles of
rotary offset features across architectures, Chiang and Yogatama
\citep{chiang2025diminefficiency} find dimensions that carry position without
helping retrieval, and He and colleagues \citep{he2025lowrank} build a low-rank
sparse replacement for attention layers. Each concerns \emph{how much}
position a channel carries, not the sign of what it carries. The spectral view
belongs to Xiong et al. \citep{xiong2025dope}, \citet{li2026fingerprint}, and
frequency-band control in shared-attention DiTs
\citep{mikaeili2026untwistingrope}.

\textbf{Position-blindness is a known gap, but not this one.} Haklay and
colleagues \citep{haklay2025positionaware} observe that circuit-discovery
methods assume position-invariance and introduce position-aware edge
attribution to relax it, which is the strongest existing support for our
framing. It is a different blindness: they distinguish \emph{which token
position} a component matters at, whereas we are concerned with the score's
geometric dependence on the distance between two positions.

\textbf{Our relationship to the attribution literature.} Those methods ask
which features caused a behaviour in a trained model. We ask a prior question
about the position encoding alone: given a RoPE head, is a feature's
contribution a scalar? Our answer --- no, it is a function of distance --- is a
structural precondition any of those methods must handle. Our per-distance
attribution has not been connected to a circuit, and we have not shown that
measuring it identifies anything about a trained model.

\textbf{What exactness does not buy.} A faithful decomposition is not a
validated circuit. \citet{zhang2026circuiteval} show that circuits can closely
reproduce a model's successful decisions while accounting for only a minority of
its errors, so agreement on successes is not sufficient evidence of a mechanism.
At the behavioural level, \citet{enouen2026shifting} find that the choice of
positional encoding changes whether in-context retrieval relies on positional
or semantic mechanisms across a suite of open-weight models. That is a downstream
claim, and this paper makes none: we establish what the score attribution must
represent, not which behaviour it explains. Exactness removes one structural
objection to a RoPE attribution; it does not supply the circuit or behavioural
validation those works ask for.

\paragraph{The maximum angle is not changed by \yarn.}
$\max_k|\D|$ for RoPE and \yarn\ is equal at \emph{every} distance in the grid.
At $\delta = 4096$ both are exactly $4096\rad$, and across all $54$ distances of
the finer grid the largest disagreement is $0.000\times10^{0}\rad$
(Figure~\ref{app:fig:maxmedian}). This is not an accident of rounding: \yarn's
ramp is constructed to leave short-wavelength channels at their original inverse
frequency, so the fastest channel contributes exactly $1\rad$ per token under
both schemes. Any reasoning that says ``\yarn\ damps the large angles'' is wrong
about the largest one.

\paragraph{The median angle falls, and the linearizable fraction rises.}
$\operatorname{median}_k|\D|$ for \yarn\ is a constant $0.4464\times$ that of
RoPE at every distance tested --- $2.67$ against $5.97\rad$ at $\delta = 512$,
and $21.34$ against $47.79$ at $\delta = 4096$. A fixed ratio, not an
improvement that grows with distance, because \yarn's ramp is a fixed
per-channel rescaling and $\D$ is linear in $\delta$ for every channel.
Figure~\ref{app:fig:spectrum} shows the per-channel form of this: at
$\delta = 4493$ the median angle is $52.42\rad$ under RoPE against
$23.40\rad$ under \yarn, and the two distributions have visibly different
\emph{left} tails even though their right edges coincide exactly.
Consequently the fraction of channels admitting $\cos \D \mapsto 1 - \D^2/2$,
$\sin \D \mapsto \D$ rises from $0.0625$ to $0.3438$ at $\delta = 512$, and from
$0.0000$ to $0.2188$ at $\delta = 4096$. The base-raising heuristic is a
different row with different numbers again ($0.3438$ and $0.1875$), and
position interpolation is the most favourable on this metric because it damps
the fast channel too, at the cost of shrinking $\max_k|\D|$ from $4096$ to
$128\rad$.

\paragraph{Neither rescues a global linearization.}
Plain RoPE retains no linearizable channel at all from $\delta = 861$ onward,
where it is exactly $0.0000$ at every subsequent grid point, while \yarn\ retains
$11/32$ there and $4/32$ at $\delta = 8192$; so \yarn\ lengthens the range over
which a majority of channels can be linearized without rescuing the whole head.
The mechanism is in Figure~\ref{app:fig:pairs}: the fastest rotary pair's error
reaches the size of its own contribution at $\delta = 3$, identically under both
schemes, because \yarn\ does not touch that channel. The fastest channel turns a
revolution per token, so it leaves the linear regime within three tokens and
never returns; no amount of ramping the slow tail repairs it. The
fraction-linearizable curve and the amplitude-weighted error for all four
schemes are in Figures~\ref{app:fig:linearizable} and~\ref{app:fig:error}.

\paragraph{The damage, localized.} Figure~\ref{app:fig:pairs} gives the concrete
numbers. At $\delta = 1$ the fastest pair $k = 0$ has an exact contribution of
$-0.9525$ against a linearized $-1.0607$, a normalized error of $0.1136$; the
mid-frequency pair $k = 12$ has $4.3\times10^{-6}$ at the same distance, roughly
$26000$ times smaller. By $\delta = 3$ the fastest pair's normalized error is
$1.1099$ --- past $1$, i.e. worse than deleting the term --- while the slower
pairs remain comfortably linear: the linearization fails for the fast channels
first, and \yarn\ does not change which channels those are.
Figure~\ref{app:fig:error} is the decisive guard on this conclusion. At
$\delta = 8192$ the amplitude-weighted error is $1.24572\times10^{6}$ for RoPE
and $1.24179\times10^{6}$ for \yarn, a ratio of $0.996840$: \yarn\ buys less
than $0.4\%$. The per-pair RMS ratio is $0.999977$, i.e.\ no meaningful change.
The reason follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}: the error is dominated by
the few fast channels, whose amplitudes are comparable to the slow channels'
but whose angles are enormous, and \yarn\ scales those channels by exactly $1$.
Position interpolation does better here ($1225.06$ at $\delta = 8192$, roughly
$1000\times$ below RoPE) precisely because it also shrinks the fast channel ---
which is the same reason it compresses the spectrum into a regime a trained
model does not natively use. We do not measure that trade-off.

\section{Statistics, in full}
\label{app:stats}

\paragraph{Ranking, not only sign.} Sign is one commitment; which pair
\emph{leads} is the one a reader acts on. We ranked each head's rotary pairs by
exact contribution and by the position-free constant, per (query, key, distance)
triple on the pre-registered grid. The two name the same first pair on $49.4\%$
of the triples of \texttt{pythia-160m}, $56.6\%$ on \texttt{llama-160m} and
$60.1\%$ on \texttt{SmolLM-135M}, against $12.5\%$ and $3.1\%$ for random choice
over the $8$- and $32$-pair heads; on the larger heads the surrogate's pick
lands inside the exact top three on $77.8\%$ and $77.2\%$ of triples. First
place is what is claimed, and the position-free object misses it on about half
the votes. Per head the rate spans $7.1\%$ to $93.6\%$, so the aggregate
conceals spread rather than an all-or-nothing split; rows whose exact
contributions are identically zero cast no vote, and their count is recorded in
the artefact.

\paragraph{The grid, and the seed draw.} A denser grid misses fewer cells near
the zero crossings, so the statistic is mildly sensitive to it: the drift is
$0.2389$ across the sweep actually run, whose requested densities $9$, $17$ and
$65$ do not all survive integer rounding, so the realized span is below the
nominal $7\times$. An earlier version claimed a $14\times$ span. The seed draw
is not the limiting uncertainty: the seed-to-seed standard deviation of the
pooled rate is more than two orders of magnitude below the between-head standard
deviation of $0.0170$. Note that the llama--SmolLM gap is a tenth of a point and
not material.

\paragraph{What the interval is over.} The bootstrap resamples the $558$
layer-heads with replacement, not the $2790$ head-prompt instances and not the
per-pair cells: prompts repeat the same weights, and cells within a head share a
query and key, so those are not independent draws. The interval is therefore
conditional on the three checkpoints and on the prompt family, and it does not
cover checkpoint selection. Per-head rates are in
\texttt{results/usefulness.json}, the bootstrap in
\texttt{results/uncertainty.json} and the ranking rows in
\texttt{results/agreement.json}. The interval is quoted next to the $27.0\%$
itself, not instead of it: the two summaries answer different questions.

\section{The attention-level cost, in full}
\label{app:attention}

The main text reports four numbers. This appendix records what they were computed
on, and the checks that the reconstruction is not what produced them.

\paragraph{Setup.} Three checkpoints --- \texttt{pythia-160m} \citep{biderman2023pythia},
\texttt{llama-160m} \citep{touvron2023llama} and \texttt{SmolLM-135M} --- each
run on three original passages of $88$--$122$ tokens, over every attention head
of every layer, with the full causal key set of each query. For a query $m$ and
each rotary pair $k$, the surrogate value is the least-squares constant of
$A_k\cos(\delta\invf_k) + B_k\sin(\delta\invf_k)$ over exactly the distances
$\delta = m - n$ that query sees, which is the optimal content-only replacement
for that query; the unrotated channels are left at their exact value. Scores are
then recomputed from the surrogate and the softmax compared against the model's
own.

\paragraph{Reconstruction is not the confound.} The exact softmax matches the
model's to a maximum residual of $2.7\times10^{-6}$ on \texttt{llama-160m} and
\texttt{SmolLM-135M}, and to $9.5\times10^{-3}$ on \texttt{pythia-160m}, whose
logits saturate float32; the exact top-1 key is the
model's own for all but $1.1\%$ of \texttt{pythia-160m} queries and for every
query of the other two. The residual is a float32 effect, not a modelling error.

\paragraph{Per layer.} The argmax flip rate is $0.63$--$0.81$ across the first
two layers of the three models and averages $0.24$--$0.33$ over the remaining
layers, though individual later layers span $0.04$--$0.74$; total variation
follows the same shape. Early layers are where logits are least
saturated, so a small change in the score can move the maximum, and the aggregate
rate is dominated by them rather than uniform. Per-layer rows for all three
models, with the head-level rates, are in \texttt{results/attention\_cost.json}
(key \texttt{by\_layer}); the schema also records the per-prompt rates, so the
aggregate can be re-derived rather than trusted.

\paragraph{What the number means.} The surrogate is fitted with knowledge of the
exact profile, so no content-only method can do better on this metric. The
measured damage is therefore a lower bound on the damage of any position-free
score, and the bound is realised by a method that is allowed to cheat. It is a
statement about the score-to-weights step, not about per-feature attribution;
the per-feature cost is Section~\ref{sec:worth}.

\section{Why the withdrawn ratio measures the grid}
\label{app:ratio}

An earlier version of this paper reported a worst-feature ratio
$\max_\delta|c_i|/\min_\delta|c_i|$ of $920\times$ as its headline statistic. The
ratio is a maximum over a minimum of a continuous function evaluated on a finite
grid: as the grid densifies, $\min_\delta|c_i|$ keeps shrinking toward zero while
the numerator saturates, so the ratio grows without bound. Measured on the same
vectors at $5$, $17$, $48$, $104$, $235$ and $599$ distances it spans
$571\times$, and it is not even monotone in density. Across $12$ independent
seeds its standard deviation is $0.93\times$ its mean; the pooled variant, one
ratio over every feature at once, gives $1.13\times$. It measures the sampling,
not the function.

By contrast, the residual produced by replacing $c_i(\delta)$ with its
per-feature mean has an additive residual in the denominator, so it does not grow
without bound: it stays between $15.0$ and $15.2$ as the distance grid is made
$120\times$ denser. That is the quantity the main text uses, and the difference
between the two is the reason. Both are recomputed in
\texttt{rope\_attribution.statistics} and written to
\texttt{results/statistics.json}; the test suite pins the artefact's presence so
it cannot be quietly reinstated.

\section{A note on two corrections}
\label{app:note}

Two statements are common enough in informal write-ups of this area that we
record what we measured against them, since both are cases where the natural
explanation is wrong in an instructive way. The first is that ``RoPE breaks
bilinearity''. It does not, at the score level: doubling the query doubles the
score to a relative error of $7.4\times10^{-16}$. Any observed super-linearity
comes from changing what is being measured --- typically comparing a score
against an attention output, which involves the softmax. The second is that
``YaRN works by raising the base from $10000$ to $500000$''. It does not.
\yarn\ keeps the base and ramps per frequency, leaving the fastest $9$ of $32$
pairs bit-for-bit unchanged; the base-raising recipe is a distinct scheme again,
since it rescales channel $k$ by $s^{-2k/d}$ rather than by $s^{-1}$, and
appears as its own row in
Table~\ref{tab:spectrum}. Both corrections push the same way: toward
disentangling the position encoding from the operations people attribute to it.

\section{Scope, stated in full}
\label{app:scope}

No sparse autoencoder is trained in this work. The dictionary of
Section~\ref{sec:features} is public, was trained by its authors, and enters the
paper as a fixed object at one layer; we neither train nor fine-tune it.
Section~\ref{sec:trained} then repeats the central measurements on activations
from three pretrained checkpoints; they are used as measurement instruments and
are not evaluated on a task. There is no retrieval benchmark and no accuracy
measurement: we report no perplexity and no needle-in-a-haystack score. We
cannot and do not report anything of the form ``the model retrieves the needle
with accuracy $0.7$'', because nothing like that is measured. The checkpoints at
$135$--$160$M parameters are measurement instruments, and three checkpoints with
one prompt family and no downstream task cannot tell you whether per-distance
attribution identifies anything a circuit cares about.

\paragraph{Approximation choices.} Linearization uses $\cos\D \mapsto 1 -
\D^2/2$, $\sin\D \mapsto\D$ with a $0.1\rad$ threshold. A different truncation
or a per-channel threshold would move the linearizable fractions; we verified
the conclusion only qualitatively and did not sweep thresholds.

\paragraph{Scope of the exactness claim.} Exactness at the score level is a
statement about a bilinear map. It does not survive the softmax --- attention
\emph{weights} are not additive, so a feature's share of attention mass is not
its share of the score --- and it does not survive multi-head composition,
where the position-conditional structure is per-head. Exactness removes one
stated objection to RoPE-based attribution; it does not validate the method.
